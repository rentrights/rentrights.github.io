Subletting Your Apartment — Legal Rights, Risks & Rules

Need to travel, relocate temporarily, or cover rent while away? Subletting can solve your problem — but getting it wrong can cost you your lease. Here is exactly what the law says, state by state.

Updated: July 2026 Read time: 11 minutes Coverage: All 50 States
⚡ Quick Answer

Whether you can sublet depends on your lease and your state's law. Most leases require landlord approval for subletting — but in states like New York and California, landlords cannot unreasonably refuse a subletting request. If your lease is silent on subletting, you may have the right to sublet without asking. Always get written approval before subletting — unauthorized subletting can be grounds for eviction.

What Is Subletting — And How Is It Different from Assignment?

Subletting (also called subleasing) means temporarily renting your apartment to another person — your subtenant — while you remain legally responsible under the original lease. You collect rent from your subtenant and continue paying your landlord.

This is different from a lease assignment, where you permanently transfer your lease to someone else and step out of the picture entirely. In an assignment, the new tenant takes over all rights and responsibilities. In a sublease, you remain on the hook.

⚠️ The Most Important Thing to Understand

When you sublet, you remain fully responsible to your landlord for everything — rent, damage, lease violations. If your subtenant doesn't pay rent, you still owe it. If your subtenant damages the unit, you can be charged. Subletting does not transfer your obligations to your landlord.

Step 1 — Check Your Lease First

Your lease is the first document to consult. Leases typically fall into one of three categories regarding subletting:

Subletting Prohibited

Many leases include a clause that completely prohibits subletting without landlord consent. This is the most common type. Even here, some states give you the right to request consent anyway — and landlords cannot unreasonably withhold it.

Subletting Permitted with Landlord Approval

The lease allows subletting but requires written landlord approval first. This is the safest arrangement — you have an explicit process to follow. Always request approval in writing and keep the response.

Lease Is Silent on Subletting

If your lease doesn't mention subletting at all, the rules depend on your state. In many states, silence means you may sublet freely. In others, you still need landlord permission. Check your state's law or consult a local tenant attorney.

✅ Always Read Your Lease Carefully

Look for these words in your lease: "subletting," "sublease," "assignment," "occupancy," and "guests." Some leases restrict subletting indirectly through occupancy clauses that limit who can live in the unit. Even if "subletting" isn't mentioned, other clauses may apply.

State Laws on Subletting — Key Differences

State law significantly affects your subletting rights — sometimes overriding what your lease says. Here are the most important state-by-state differences:

When Your Landlord Refuses Your Subletting Request

If you live in a state where landlords cannot unreasonably refuse subletting, their reasons for refusal must be legitimate. Acceptable reasons generally include:

  • The proposed subtenant fails a standard credit or background check
  • The proposed subtenant has a history of property damage or evictions
  • The sublease would violate local occupancy limits
  • The proposed subtenant refuses to complete a standard rental application

Reasons that are generally NOT acceptable for refusal:

  • Simply not wanting a subtenant without a specific reason
  • Wanting to re-rent the unit at a higher market rate
  • Discriminatory reasons (race, national origin, family status, etc.)
  • Retaliation for exercising tenant rights
🚨 If Refused Unreasonably in NY or NJ

In New York and New Jersey, if your landlord unreasonably refuses a subletting request, you may have the right to sublet anyway — and the landlord's unreasonable refusal may be used as a defense if they try to evict you for subletting. Document your request and their refusal carefully. Consult a local tenant attorney before proceeding.

How to Sublet Safely — Step by Step

1

Review Your Lease Thoroughly

Read every clause related to subletting, assignment, occupancy, and guests. Note any restrictions, notice requirements, or approval processes specified in your lease.

2

Request Written Landlord Approval

Send a written request to your landlord by email and certified mail. Include: your subtenant's name, proposed sublease dates, and a brief background on the subtenant. Keep copies of everything.

3

Screen Your Subtenant Carefully

You are responsible for your subtenant's behavior. Run a credit check, verify income, check references, and ask about employment. A bad subtenant can cost you your lease and your security deposit.

4

Sign a Written Sublease Agreement

Never sublet without a written agreement. Your sublease should cover: rent amount and due date, sublease term, deposit paid by subtenant, rules they must follow (your lease rules apply to them), and what happens at the end of the sublease.

5

Document the Unit's Condition

Do a thorough photo walkthrough with your subtenant before they move in — just like your move-in with your landlord. This protects you from being charged for damage they cause.

6

Collect a Security Deposit from Subtenant

Collect a deposit from your subtenant to cover potential damage or unpaid rent. Your state's security deposit laws may apply to this sublease deposit — check your state's rules on how to hold and return it.

Sublease Agreement — Key Elements

Every sublease should be in writing and signed by both parties. Here are the essential elements:

📄 Sublease Agreement — Essential Sections

SUBLEASE AGREEMENT

This Sublease Agreement is entered into between [Your Name] ("Sublessor") and [Subtenant Name] ("Sublessee") for the property located at [Property Address].

Term: This sublease begins on [Start Date] and ends on [End Date]. Sublessee must vacate by the end date.

Rent: Sublessee agrees to pay $[Amount] per month, due on the [Day] of each month, payable to Sublessor by [payment method].

Security Deposit: Sublessee has paid $[Amount] as a security deposit. This will be returned within [X] days of vacating, minus any lawful deductions.

Lease Compliance: Sublessee agrees to comply with all terms of the original lease between Sublessor and landlord [Landlord Name], a copy of which has been provided to Sublessee. Any violation of the original lease by Sublessee may result in termination of this sublease.

No Further Subletting: Sublessee may not sublet the unit to any other party without Sublessor's written consent.

Condition of Unit: Sublessee acknowledges receiving the unit in the condition documented in the attached move-in photos dated [Date].

Signed: [Sublessor Signature, Date] · [Sublessee Signature, Date]

Risks of Unauthorized Subletting

Subletting without permission — when your lease requires it — is a serious lease violation. The consequences can include:

  • Lease termination — your landlord can issue a notice to cure or quit, and ultimately evict you
  • Eviction of your subtenant — they can be removed without your involvement
  • Liability for subtenant's actions — you remain fully responsible for any damage or unpaid rent
  • Loss of security deposit — unauthorized subletting may be cited as cause for withholding
  • Legal action — in some cases landlords can sue for damages caused by unauthorized subtenants
✅ Always Get Written Approval

Even if you believe your landlord will say yes, always get approval in writing before your subtenant moves in. A verbal "yes" from a landlord is nearly impossible to prove and won't protect you if they later claim they never approved the sublease.
