Eviction Process Timeline — How Long Does Eviction Take?

Facing eviction is terrifying — but understanding every step of the process gives you the time and information you need to protect yourself. Here is exactly what happens, when, and what your rights are at each stage.

Updated: July 2026 Read time: 12 minutes Coverage: All 50 States
⚡ Quick Answer

A full eviction — from first notice to physical removal — takes 3 to 12 weeks in most US states, depending on the state and whether you contest the eviction. No landlord can legally remove you without a court order. The process has required steps that take time — understanding them is your strongest tool.

The Eviction Process — 5 Required Stages

In every US state, a landlord must follow a specific legal process to evict a tenant. There are no shortcuts. A landlord who tries to remove you without following this process is committing an illegal "self-help eviction" — which is a serious legal violation in all 50 states.

Understanding this process gives you time — time to pay rent, find a new place, fix a lease violation, or prepare a legal defense. Here are the five required stages:

1
Stage 1 · Day 1–30

Written Notice to Vacate

The eviction process begins with a written notice from your landlord. The type and length of notice depends on the reason for eviction. You have the right to respond — and in some cases, paying rent or fixing the violation ends the process here.

✓ You can cure most violations at this stage
2
Stage 2 · Week 1–3

Landlord Files Eviction Lawsuit

If you don't comply with the notice, the landlord files an eviction complaint in court (called "Unlawful Detainer," "Forcible Entry and Detainer," or "Summary Possession" depending on state). You are served with court papers and given time to respond.

✓ You must respond in writing to avoid default
3
Stage 3 · Week 2–6

Court Hearing

Both sides present their case to a judge. You can raise defenses, present evidence, and challenge the landlord's claims. This is your most important opportunity. Many eviction cases are decided at this stage.

✓ Bring all evidence — you can win here
4
Stage 4 · After Hearing

Judgment and Appeal

The judge issues a ruling. If the landlord wins, they receive a "writ of possession." You typically have 5–30 days (depending on state) to appeal. Filing an appeal keeps you in the unit during the appeal period.

✓ You can appeal — this buys more time
5
Stage 5 · Final Stage

Writ of Possession & Physical Removal

Only after all legal options are exhausted can a sheriff or marshal physically remove you. This is the final stage. Even here, many states require 24-48 hours notice before the lockout occurs.

✓ You still have rights — your belongings are protected
⚠️ Never Ignore Court Papers

Failing to respond to eviction court papers almost always results in a default judgment — meaning the landlord wins automatically without presenting any evidence. Even if you plan to move out, always file a written response to protect your rights and rental record.

Notice Types — What Your Notice Means

The type of notice you receive tells you exactly why the eviction is being pursued and what options you have:

Pay or Quit Notice (3–5 Days)

You owe unpaid rent. This is the most common eviction notice. In most states you have 3–5 days to pay the full amount owed. If you pay within this window, the eviction process stops immediately. Even if the deadline has passed, many landlords will accept payment before the court date.

Cure or Quit Notice (3–30 Days)

You violated a lease term (unauthorized pet, unauthorized occupant, etc.) and have a set number of days to fix ("cure") the violation. If you cure the violation within the notice period, the eviction stops. If you can't or won't cure, the landlord can proceed to file in court.

Unconditional Quit Notice (3–30 Days)

The landlord demands you leave with no option to pay or cure. This is used for serious or repeated violations — significant damage, illegal activity, or repeated lease violations. No opportunity to fix the problem is given.

Month-to-Month Termination (30–90 Days)

The landlord is ending your month-to-month tenancy — no wrongdoing required in most states. Notice period varies: 30 days in most states, 60 days if you've lived there over a year in California, up to 90 days in some jurisdictions.

Your Legal Defenses at the Eviction Hearing

At your court hearing, you can raise legal defenses that may result in the eviction being dismissed or delayed. Here are the most commonly successful defenses:

💰 Landlord Accepted Rent After Notice

If your landlord accepted rent payment after giving you a notice to vacate, they may have "waived" the eviction. Bring proof of the payment and when it was accepted.

🔧 Landlord Failed to Make Repairs

If you withheld rent because of serious habitability failures, this may be a complete defense — if you followed proper procedure. Document the conditions and your repair requests.

📋 Procedural Defects

Improper notice (wrong form, wrong delivery method, wrong timeframe) can invalidate an eviction. Review your notice against your state's exact requirements.

😤 Retaliatory Eviction

If the eviction follows your exercise of a legal right (repair request, code complaint), it may be retaliatory. Document the timeline — most states presume retaliation within 60–180 days.

📊 Rent Was Paid

If the eviction is for nonpayment and you have proof of payment, bring ALL receipts, bank statements, and payment records. A landlord who claims nonpayment despite receiving rent has a weak case.

⚖️ Fair Housing Violation

If you believe the eviction is motivated by discrimination (race, national origin, disability, etc.), this is a complete defense and a separate cause of action against the landlord.
